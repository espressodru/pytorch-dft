DeepMind published a paper in December 2021 titled "Pushing the frontiers of density functionals by solving the fractional electron problem". By training a DFT functional called DM21 under physical constraints such as fractional charge and fractional spin (FC/FS), they achieved greater accuracy than traditional functionals across multiple test sets and resolved certain systematic errors. DeepMind thus claimed that AI had, for the first time, described matter at the quantum level. However, eight months later, Igor and several other scientists published a technical commentary arguing that the original DeepMind study contained problems, specifically, that the training and test sets might overlap, rendering the experimental conclusions invalid.

Igor et al. argued as follows: First, regarding the BBB dataset, which contains dissociation curves for many dimers: DM21, under FC/FS constraints, was able to predict dissociation curves more accurately. But this may be due to a prosaic reason. The end of each dissociation curve in BBB corresponds to complete separation of the dimer, which is consistent with the FC/FS training data. Thus, although the BBB dissociation curves did not formally appear in the training set, their critical limiting states were already present in the training data. Moreover, by comparing the density of the dimer at separation with the FC density used by DM21, one finds that the two are already very similar even at intermediate distances—long before reaching the long-distance limit. Regarding the adenine–thymine (A–T) base pair example: the original DM21 paper claimed to correctly predict a density distribution without charge delocalization. However, hydrogen bonds exist between A and T, so complete absence of charge delocalization is impossible. Regarding the H₂₄ example: the original DM21 paper claimed that for a compressed H₂₄ chain, DM21 obtained a spin-symmetric solution, whereas high-level wave-function methods did not yield a spin-polarized solution. Upon examining DM21m (the version trained without FC/FS constraints), both symmetry-preserved and broken-symmetry solutions were found. Which solution is obtained depends on the starting guess. Therefore, the SCF does not necessarily have only one stable solution, and there is insufficient evidence that the true ground state must be symmetry-preserved. It may simply be a stationary point, and its energy is 44.4 kcal/mol higher than the broken-symmetry solution! For bicyclobutane, DM21 does perform better than DM21m on the two conrotatory transition states. However, on the disrotatory TS—which theoretically has higher nondynamical correlation and should better exemplify the strong-correlation problem. The two are almost equally good. Since DM21m was never trained with FS/FC data yet can still handle this high nondynamical-correlation problem, DM21's advantage on the conrotatory TS cannot be simply attributed to "FS/FC training teaching it strong correlation." The SIE4x4 dataset also contains data that overlaps with the long-distance ends of the BBB dissociation curves, and thus also appears in DM21's FS/FC training data. DM21's error actually increases with distance. This is notable because the fractional-charge / self-interaction problem typically becomes more pronounced as fragment separation increases. So although DM21 is better than DM21m, it does not exhibit a very clean, perfect FC behavior. More critically, DM21 was ultimately surpassed by another functional, mKP16/B13.

DeepMind promptly published a rebuttal, standing by the paper's conclusions. On the BBB dataset: even if DM21 had seen the FC/FS atoms at infinite separation, it had not "memorized" the entire BBB dissociation curve. If that were truly the case, the exchange-correlation energy Exc should rapidly approach a fixed FC limit as R increases. In reality, Exc(R) varies significantly across the entire BBB distance range. DeepMind specifically noted that the opponents incorrectly used the fractional occupation curve, whereas DM21 on BBB uses integer occupation, not fractional occupation, a difference that affects their computed assessments. On Adenine–Thymine: DeepMind stated that they were not using A–T at any finite distance at all. They used A–T at infinite separation, where fractional electron transfer has strict meaning. On H₂₄: DeepMind said they re-examined H₂₄ and indeed performed optimizations starting from a broken-symmetry initial guess. Specifically: the initial condition was BS; DM21 itself optimized to SP; DM21m behaved differently. Therefore, it is not a case of "you gave it SP at the start, so you got SP at the end." On Bicyclobutane: DeepMind responded that looking only at "how high the nondynamical correlation is" is insufficient—one must also examine whether spin symmetry breaking occurs in the unrestricted Kohn–Sham (UKS) framework. DeepMind pointed out that ⟨S²⟩ ≈ 1 indicates complete spin symmetry breaking. Thus, ordinary hybrid functionals combined with UKS can already "bypass" the need to directly describe static correlation via symmetry breaking—much like stretched H₂. Therefore, high nondynamical correlation does not automatically mean one must rely on DM21's FS/FC training. In the conrotatory TS, by contrast, ⟨S²⟩ < 1, meaning spin symmetry breaking is incomplete. In this regime, traditional functionals including DM21m tend to overestimate the barrier, whereas DM21 performs better. Thus, DM21's real advantage is not in "systems with stronger correlation," but in those exhibiting "moderate spin contamination / partial symmetry breaking." On SIE4x4: mKP16/B13 = 4.71 kcal/mol; DM21 = 4.92 kcal/mol. So DM21 was not "far surpassed": 4.92 ≈ 4.71, a very small gap. DM21m = 8.43 kcal/mol, while DM21 = 4.92 kcal/mol. Thus, adding FC training reduced the error from 8.43 to 4.92, nearly halving it. This precisely demonstrates that fractional-charge training is very important for SIE4x4. Moreover, DM21 also performs well on data in SIE4x4 that is completely different from BBB, indicating that it is not mere memorization.

However, both sides agree on the validity of using physical constraints for DFT training, especially training under the uniform electron gas constraint. Next, I plan to use this approach combined with traditional algorithms to study specific problems such as photosynthesis and superconductivity.
